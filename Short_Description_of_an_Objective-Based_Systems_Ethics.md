# How Objective-Based Systems Ethics Works

![Objective-Based Systems Ethics: Runtime Assessment Model. The diagram shows consequential world-transition evaluation, the three-tier flourishing hierarchy of Biosphere, Society, and Individual, a hybrid deployment pattern, and the seven-step moral assessment workflow.](media/Objective-Based_Systems_Ethics_Runtime_001.png)

Every moral choice changes the world. Some changes appear at once. Others appear years later, in trust, in institutions, and in what people come to treat as normal. Objective-Based Systems Ethics is a way to look at that change directly, and to say — in a form a person or a machine can inspect — whether the world that follows is one in which life can keep flourishing.

The model begins with a picture of the relevant world: the beings involved, their relationships, the institutions, the limits they face, what is known, and the futures still open. That picture is the world context. An action is judged by the transition it makes, from the world before the action to the world after it.

The direction of the judgment is stated in the open, so anyone can challenge it. A moral system, on this account, preserves and promotes the long-term flourishing of beings that persist and can adapt — people, other organisms, ecosystems, and, where they qualify, future artificial systems — together with the contexts that make that flourishing possible. Those contexts sit in three tiers: the living world, society, and the individual. A person flourishes fully only inside a flourishing society and a living world, and that person's own conduct sustains or damages those contexts in return. When the tiers truly conflict, the broader one prevails, because damage there spreads to everything it supports. That priority ranks enabling conditions, not moral worth. A whole is not flourishing if its parts are being worn down to secure it.

The means belong in the judgment even when the immediate result looks good. An action also updates the policies by which the actor, the observers, the institutions, and any artificial systems will decide next time. A deception that saves an organization today can teach everyone watching that deception is how problems get solved. That lesson becomes part of the next world. A harmful result is also not, by itself, a moral failure. A careful driver who cannot avoid an animal is a different case from a driver who has already chosen not to pay attention.

An assessment follows a short path. Name the relevant world, and mark what is left out. Identify who can flourish, who is acting, and what they are trying to do. State the action and the real alternatives. Estimate how the world changes over the next hour, the coming years, and, when it matters, generations, and say how confident that estimate is. Then ask whether the resulting world better preserves and promotes flourishing without materially diminishing the flourishing of others.

A real conflict stays a conflict, with the reasons still attached. Uncertainty is carried along. The world that results becomes the starting point of the next decision.

The same structure can guide a person, a court, a hospital, a city, or an automated system. It leaves disagreement in place, and gives that disagreement a shared map: explicit assumptions, a visible change in the world, and a judgment that can be traced, challenged, and revised.
